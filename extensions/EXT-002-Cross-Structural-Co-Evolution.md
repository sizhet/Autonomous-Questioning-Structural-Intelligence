# EXT-002 — Cross-Structural Co-Evolution

**Project:** Autonomous Questioning Structural Intelligence (AQSI)  
**Document type:** Research Extension / Directional Hypothesis  
**Version:** 1.0.0  
**Status:** Theory-first proposal; mechanisms and benefits require independent validation  
**License:** Apache-2.0 (repository-level)

## Abstract

Autonomous Questioning Structural Intelligence (AQSI) does not operate in isolation. Its question discovery, structural representation, historical scoring, research governance, and exploration processes depend on surrounding infrastructures for naming, typing, alignment, evidence management, scoring, relay, and controlled structural growth. Conversely, questions generated by AQSI may expose deficiencies in those infrastructures: missing Universal Typing and Naming (UTN) types, inadequate influence operators, insufficient provenance contracts, or incomplete Structural Relay interfaces. This extension proposes **Cross-Structural Co-Evolution (CSCE)** as a research framework for examining how improvements in one structural intelligence subsystem can create measurable opportunities—or new risks—in others.

The proposed framework distinguishes *cross-structural interaction*, *candidate adaptation*, *verified local improvement*, and *sustained co-evolution*. It introduces typed change proposals, dependency-aware impact analysis, bounded experimentation, compatibility contracts, counter-evidence, rollback, and multi-perspective governance. AQSI, UTN, Question Differential Trees, multi-granularity influence scoring, Historical Question Intelligence, UCPSF-style folding, SIF localization, UIERU-style relay, and structural recursive self-improvement are treated as interoperable research components rather than assumed implementations. The central hypothesis is that carefully governed cross-system feedback may improve the quality, portability, and cumulative value of research questions compared with isolated component optimization. No such improvement is claimed as established.

**Keywords:** structural intelligence; autonomous questioning; co-evolution; UTN; multi-granularity influence; historical question intelligence; structural relay; conformance; controlled recursive improvement; collective learning.

## 1. Motivation and Scope

The preceding AQSI documents establish a pipeline for discovering candidate questions, representing them as typed Question Units, estimating directional influence, selecting portfolios, conducting authorized explorations, and relaying outcomes. Each stage makes assumptions about other structural systems. For example:

- Question discovery assumes that relevant gaps and residuals can be identified.
- Influence scoring assumes that heterogeneous factors can be aligned or explicitly marked as incomparable.
- Historical reuse assumes that a past question's context, methods, and outcome can be retrieved without erasing uncertainty.
- Relay assumes that recipients can interpret the transferred structure and its provenance.
- Governance assumes that policy and authorization boundaries remain enforceable across adaptations.

A deficiency in one component can therefore affect several others. A new UTN relation type may make a previously invisible cross-domain question expressible; the resulting exploration may reveal that the type is too broad; a refined type may improve historical retrieval, which changes future question portfolios. This is a plausible **feedback mechanism**, not proof of beneficial evolution.

This paper asks: **Can structural intelligence components improve one another through traceable, experimentally controlled feedback, without losing semantic integrity or human governance?**

### 1.1 Scope

This extension focuses on (a) cross-component change proposals, (b) representation and dependency contracts, (c) measurable interactions, (d) experiments and falsifiers, and (e) governance of accepted changes. It does not define a universal runtime, claim a complete theory of intelligence, or treat self-modification as intrinsically valuable.

### 1.2 Terminology

- **Interaction:** one component consumes or produces artifacts used by another.
- **Adaptation:** a proposed change to a component, schema, model, policy, or interface.
- **Local improvement:** a change that passes a specified evaluation on a particular task distribution.
- **Co-evolution:** a sequence of interacting changes across at least two components, with measured effects and retained provenance.
- **Verified co-evolution:** co-evolution that passes prespecified cross-component tests and independent evaluation; it is not synonymous with open-ended progress.

## 3. The Cross-Structural Dependency Graph

Let the research system at time \(t\) be a typed graph:

\[
\mathcal{G}_t=(\mathcal{V}_t,\mathcal{E}_t,\mathcal{C}_t,\mathcal{P}_t),
\]

where \(\mathcal{V}_t\) contains components, contracts, artifacts, and evaluators; \(\mathcal{E}_t\) contains typed dependencies and influence claims; \(\mathcal{C}_t\) contains compatibility and conformance requirements; and \(\mathcal{P}_t\) contains provenance and governance metadata.

An edge \(e_{ab}\) from component \(a\) to component \(b\) may mean **depends on**, **supplies evidence to**, **changes interpretation of**, **constrains**, or **proposes revision to**. These are not interchangeable relations. UTN-style type contracts should identify the intended meaning, source context, validity interval, and permitted consumers.

### 3.1 Multi-granularity dependencies

A dependency may concern an entire subsystem or a smaller factor: a relation type, score calibration, prompt expression, confidence interval, schema field, evaluator, or evidence source. Thus the graph is not restricted to one node per research framework.

### 3.2 Directionality and uncertainty

A proposed change to UTN may alter AQSI retrieval while a change to AQSI may not alter UTN at all. Therefore:

\[
D(a\!\rightarrow\!b)\neq D(b\!\rightarrow\!a)
\]

in general. Missing dependency evidence is **unknown**, not a zero-effect claim. Uncertainty must remain attached to both the edge and the estimate.

### 3.3 Versioned semantics

A component reference should include a stable identifier, semantic version or content digest, declared compatibility range, and explicit migration mapping where necessary. A reused identifier must not silently change meaning.

## 4. A Candidate Co-Evolution Loop

The following is a proposed research cycle, not an observed autonomous capability.

1. **Observe:** AQSI or another participant detects a gap, failure, mismatch, unexplained residual, or opportunity.
2. **Formulate:** Create a Question Unit asking whether a component adaptation would address the observation.
3. **Localize:** Identify affected contracts, types, evaluators, and downstream consumers.
4. **Propose:** Construct a versioned Structural Change Proposal (SCP) with alternatives and explicit unknowns.
5. **Estimate:** Predict directional cross-component effects, including possible harms and costs.
6. **Authorize:** Obtain the permissions required for the proposed sandbox experiment.
7. **Test:** Compare candidate and baseline against predeclared metrics and counterexamples.
8. **Review:** Evaluate evidence, negative transfer, regressions, and externalities.
9. **Adopt or reject:** Apply an authorized, reversible change or preserve a rejected/deferred proposal.
10. **Relay:** Feed outcomes, evidence, and new questions into AQSI, HQI, and the dependency graph.

The loop is *closed* only if observed outcomes influence subsequent decisions. Merely generating a proposal and storing it does not establish learning.

## 5. Structural Change Proposal (SCP)

A minimal SCP should make the proposed adaptation inspectable without requiring execution of untrusted instructions.

```yaml
scp_id: "scp:aqsi:illustrative:001"
status: proposed
origin_question: "qu:aqsi:illustrative:041"
target:
  component: "utn:relation-registry"
  version: "example-v1"
change:
  kind: "add-relation-subtype"
  summary: "Distinguish method-transfer from evidence-transfer"
  semantic_contract: "example:method-transfer-contract-v1"
assumptions:
  - "Existing source/target types remain distinguishable"
affected_consumers:
  - "aqsi:influence-scoring"
  - "aqsi:historical-retrieval"
expected_effects:
  - metric: "cross-domain-transfer-precision"
    direction: "increase-hypothesis"
  - metric: "schema-complexity"
    direction: "increase-risk"
unknowns:
  - "Impact on sparsely observed domains"
evidence_refs: []
counter_evidence_refs: []
experiment:
  baseline: "current-registry"
  candidate: "sandbox-registry"
  acceptance_rule: "pre-registered; not supplied by this example"
rollback:
  strategy: "restore previous registry and mappings"
authorization:
  state: "not-authorized"
provenance:
  origin: "illustrative-example-not-observed"
```

The example is schematic. It is not a deployed schema, an approved change, or a measured result.

### 5.1 Mandatory SCP fields

Every actionable proposal should include: identity; source question; target and version; change semantics; expected and adverse effects; assumptions; evidence and counterevidence; affected consumers; authorization requirements; experiment design; acceptance rule; rollback; and provenance. A system may extend this contract but should not omit its governance-critical fields.

## 6. AQSI ↔ UTN: Typed Discovery and Type Evolution

AQSI can identify a question whose source and destination factors appear related but cannot be mapped under existing UTN types. This observation may generate a **type-gap question**, for example: *Does the current taxonomy incorrectly collapse method transfer and evidence transfer?*

The proposed workflow is:

1. Preserve the failed alignment as a typed residual.
2. Gather examples and counterexamples from multiple domains.
3. Compare candidate subtype, mapping, and no-change alternatives.
4. Test retrieval and transfer behavior under each alternative.
5. Require explicit review of compatibility, false alignments, and ontology proliferation.
6. Accept a versioned UTN change only if its measured value justifies its complexity.

The reverse direction is equally important: new UTN types may expose previously unaddressable questions. **Type proliferation is not progress by itself.** A more expressive ontology may worsen retrieval or introduce spurious matches.

## 7. AQSI ↔ Multi-Granularity Influence Scoring

A newly discovered question may reveal a factor missing from the influence model. Conversely, the model may detect an unexplained high-influence structural link and propose a new question.

For question \(q_i\), let \(H(q_i)=\{h_{i1},\ldots,h_{im}\}\) be its heterogeneous typed factors. A candidate directional influence estimator is:

\[
\widehat I_t(q_i\rightarrow q_j)
=\operatorname{Fold}_{\phi_t}
\left(\{\widehat R_t(h_{ia},h_{jb}),w_{ab,t},u_{ab,t}\}_{a,b}\right),
\]

where \(\widehat R_t\) is a typed relation estimate, \(w_{ab,t}\) is a context-dependent weight, \(u_{ab,t}\) represents uncertainty or missingness, and \(\phi_t\) specifies the folding contract.

A proposed change to the factor vocabulary, relation model, or folding operator must be tested for:

- predictive calibration on held-out question pairs;
- sensitivity to graph density and redundant factors;
- directionality preservation;
- robustness to missing/unknown relations;
- subgroup and cross-domain error;
- downstream portfolio decisions and research costs.

Do not equate higher estimated influence with verified research value. Historical outcomes and independent exploration remain necessary.

## 8. AQSI ↔ Historical Question Intelligence and QDT

Historical Question Intelligence (HQI) may improve screening by learning from previously explored questions. But a revised type system or Question Differential Tree changes the representation used to retrieve those histories. This creates **representation–retrieval feedback**.

A controlled update should preserve old versions, compute new representations alongside them, and compare performance on time-split and domain-held-out data. Historical outcomes should retain observation dates, evidence quality, delayed descendants, costs, and unresolved status. A question that produced no immediate answer may later have generated a valuable method or downstream question.

**Selection bias** is a central hazard: only selected questions receive extensive exploration, so their outcomes are not a representative sample of all candidates. Evaluation should use controlled exploration allocations, logged selection propensities where available, and uncertainty-aware estimates; none fully eliminates confounding by default.

QDT co-evolution can involve refining a shared question Core, adding domain Delta branches, or splitting a misleading family. Every change should be checked against semantic drift and the loss of earlier counterexamples.

## 9. AQSI ↔ Structural Relay and Collective Learning

Relay is the mechanism by which a local discovery can become reusable elsewhere. Candidate relay pieces include:

- typed question and question-family deltas;
- evidence and counterevidence packages;
- proposed relation or ontology changes;
- scoring and evaluator revisions;
- failed experiment records and Explicit Leftover;
- migration and compatibility mappings;
- provenance-preserving repeat-execution records.

A recipient must be able to **inspect, decline, quarantine, test, or adapt** a relay piece. Receipt is not adoption; adoption is not verification. Cross-domain transfer requires target-domain conformance and independent evaluation.

### 9.1 Repeated execution as relay

Repeated runs can accumulate evidence about variability, reproducibility, drift, and cost. They contribute new information only to the extent that the run context, dependencies, and observations are recorded and the evidence is not double-counted as independent.

### 9.2 Collective learning criterion

A candidate operational criterion is that a relay artifact measurably changes a later participant's research decision or validated outcome, with traceable attribution. Mere message exchange or artifact accumulation is insufficient.

## 10. AQSI ↔ SIF, UIERU, and Controlled SRSI

**SIF-style localization** may identify the capability or structural node relevant to a question. An unresolvable question may reveal a missing capability, leading to a proposed new component or interface.

**UIERU-style relay** may package the resulting question, method, evidence, and compatibility constraints as a portable research unit. Portability requires explicit contracts and destination-side validation, not just serialization.

**Controlled structural recursive self-improvement** may test changes to AQSI's discovery operators, scorers, historical retrieval, or relay policies. The governing principle is:

> A question about improving the system is not itself a system improvement; a plausible modification is not an accepted modification; an accepted modification is not evidence of unbounded recursive progress.

All model and policy changes should pass predefined conformance and safety checks. The strategic control plane retains authority over budgets, permitted actions, and adoption of governance-affecting modifications.

## 11. Multi-Perspective Co-Evolution Scoring

A proposed change \(\Delta\) may improve one subsystem while harming another. Its evaluation should therefore be a vector rather than an unquestioned scalar:

\[
\mathbf{E}(\Delta)=
(\Delta Q,\Delta T,\Delta C,\Delta R,\Delta G,\Delta K),
\]

where the coordinates represent, respectively, question quality, transfer quality, computational or human cost, reliability, governance conformance, and compatibility. Each coordinate requires an explicit metric and direction convention; no universal weighting is assumed.

A policy-approved aggregator may be used for a specific decision:

\[
U_{\pi}(\Delta)=\operatorname{Aggregate}_{\pi}(\mathbf{E}(\Delta),\mathbf{u}(\Delta)),
\]

where \(\pi\) denotes authorized priorities and constraints, and \(\mathbf{u}\) contains uncertainty estimates. Hard constraints—such as forbidden actions or failed provenance requirements—must not be traded away by a high aggregate score.

### 11.1 Interaction effects

For changes \(\Delta_a\) and \(\Delta_b\), a hypothetical interaction term is:

\[
J(\Delta_a,\Delta_b)=
U(\Delta_a\oplus\Delta_b)-U(\Delta_a)-U(\Delta_b)+U(\varnothing).
\]

Positive \(J\) suggests complementarity *under the chosen metric and experiment*; negative \(J\) suggests interference. The quantity is not inherently causal without an appropriate experimental design.

### 11.2 The improvement unit

Report both component-level and system-level outcomes. A local scorer improvement may degrade the selected question portfolio, and an apparently beneficial transfer may increase downstream validation cost. The portfolio and research ecosystem are therefore relevant evaluation units.

## 12. Compatibility, Conformance, and Rollback

Cross-structural evolution introduces failure modes absent from isolated component updates. Minimum safeguards include:

1. **Schema conformance:** typed fields and version constraints are satisfied.
2. **Semantic conformance:** unchanged identifiers retain meaning; changed meanings receive migrations.
3. **Provenance integrity:** source artifacts and transformations remain auditable.
4. **Evidence integrity:** estimated, observed, replicated, and disputed claims are distinguished.
5. **Authorization:** a proposal does not confer permission to execute or deploy.
6. **Dependency impact:** downstream consumers and contracts are identified.
7. **Regression tests:** prior representative workloads are re-evaluated.
8. **Rollback readiness:** previous behavior and mappings can be restored where technically feasible.
9. **Quarantine:** untrusted relay pieces are not executed merely because they were received.
10. **Explicit Leftover:** unresolved compatibility and evaluation issues remain visible.

When a change is irreversible, the SCP must state that limitation before authorization. An unexplained failure to roll back is itself a reportable outcome.

## 13. Illustrative Case A: A Missing UTN Transfer Relation

**Scenario (hypothetical):** AQSI observes that questions about transferring debugging methods between CI pipelines and environmental sensor systems are frequently conflated with questions about transferring evidence.

**Candidate adaptation:** UTN distinguishes `method-transfer` from `evidence-transfer` with typed source/target and validation obligations.

**Expected potential benefit:** fewer false analogies in cross-domain question retrieval.

**Risks:** greater schema complexity, sparse training examples, and reduced recall.

**Experiment:** compare baseline and revised registries on held-out annotated transfer cases; measure precision, recall, downstream question utility, and reviewer time. Include domains absent from the design examples.

**Relay:** store accepted and rejected mappings, counterexamples, and newly generated questions. No positive result is assumed.

---

## 14. Illustrative Case B: Scoring–Portfolio Feedback

**Scenario (hypothetical):** A new multi-granularity factor increases estimated influence for a cluster of questions. The portfolio selector consequently concentrates resources on that cluster.

**Potential failure:** the factor captures shared vocabulary rather than genuine transfer utility, producing correlated selection errors.

**Co-evolution candidate:** revise the influence operator to track factor redundancy and evidence dependence; adapt portfolio diversity constraints only if authorized.

**Experiment:** compare baseline, scorer-only change, portfolio-only change, and combined change on a common frozen candidate set. Assess calibration, realized exploration yield, resource concentration, and adverse outcomes.

This factorial design can help distinguish component effects from interactions, although interpretation still depends on adequate sample sizes and stable evaluation conditions.

---

## 15. Illustrative Case C: Meta-Questions About the Relay Protocol

**Scenario (hypothetical):** A receiving research agent repeatedly rejects otherwise useful question packages because evidence provenance cannot be interpreted.

AQSI generates a meta-question: *Would a revised relay evidence contract reduce rejection without weakening verification?*

The candidate adaptation adds a typed provenance chain and evidence independence metadata. A sandbox comparison measures successful destination-side validation, false acceptance, transfer latency, and review burden. A successful contract revision can generate new questions about migration costs and legacy artifacts.

The loop illustrates how research problems may arise from the infrastructure of research itself.

## 16. MVP Research Architecture

The initial implementation should be deliberately narrow. A suggested MVP uses three participants: AQSI Question Registry, UTN-like Type Registry, and Historical Scoring/Relay Registry. A fourth, independent evaluator records experiment outcomes.

```text
Observed Gap / Residual / Failed Alignment
                |
                v
        AQSI Question Unit
                |
                v
      Structural Change Proposal
                |
                v
    Dependency + Conformance Check
                |
                v
     Authorized Sandbox Evaluation
                |
          +-----+------+
          |            |
       Accept       Reject/Defer
          |            |
          +-----+------+
                v
   Evidence + Counterevidence + Leftover
                |
                v
       Historical / Relay Update
                |
                v
         Next Question Cycle
```

### M0 — Observation-only

Collect typed dependencies, historical change records, failed alignments, and meta-questions. Do not permit autonomous component modification.

### M1 — Proposal and offline evaluation

Generate SCPs and evaluate them against frozen baselines, without deployment. Record prediction errors and negative results.

### M2 — Bounded multi-component experiments

Permit controlled, reversible changes to non-governance components in a sandbox. Evaluate interactions and destination-side conformance.

### M3 — Governed iterative adaptation

Allow authorized adoption after independent review, monitor drift, and preserve rollback and audit records. Progression between stages is conditional on evidence, not elapsed time.

## 17. Experimental Design and Baselines

At minimum, compare:

| Baseline | Description | Main contrast |
|---|---|---|
| B0 | Static components, no cross-feedback | Is co-evolution useful at all? |
| B1 | AQSI-only question refinement | Value beyond local questioning |
| B2 | UTN-only ontology updates | Value beyond type maintenance |
| B3 | Independent per-component tuning | Value of coupled feedback |
| B4 | Ungoverned coupled proposals in sandbox | Cost of missing control/verification |
| B5 | Human-curated change proposals | Human–AI complementarity |
| B6 | Coupled proposals without historical feedback | Contribution of HQI |
| B7 | Coupled proposals without provenance-aware relay | Contribution of relay contracts |

B4 is a **sandbox-only research comparator** and must not be deployed where unsafe or unauthorized changes could affect real systems.

### 17.1 Primary measurements

- Held-out question utility assessed independently of proposal generators.
- Cross-domain transfer precision and failure rate.
- Historical retrieval accuracy and calibration.
- Verified change acceptance rate and regression incidence.
- Downstream portfolio yield per unit cost.
- Compatibility breakage and rollback success.
- Evidence traceability and reviewer disagreement.
- Diversity of explored questions and survival of novel hypotheses.

### 17.2 Protocol safeguards

Use preregistered acceptance criteria, time-split histories, domain-held-out tests, independent annotation where feasible, and ablation studies. Report confidence intervals, negative outcomes, distribution shifts, and all attempted changes, not only accepted ones.

## 18. Testable Hypotheses

The following are hypotheses, not findings.

**H1 — Type-gap discovery:** AQSI-generated type-gap questions improve identification of useful UTN refinements over manually sampled change requests under matched review budgets.

**H2 — Cross-component benefit:** Governed coupled adaptation improves held-out question utility relative to independently tuned components at comparable cost.

**H3 — Directional dependencies:** Typed directional dependency estimates predict downstream regression risk better than undirected component adjacency alone.

**H4 — Historical feedback:** Including historical question outcomes reduces repeated low-yield change proposals without disproportionately suppressing genuinely novel candidates.

**H5 — Contract preservation:** Explicit compatibility and provenance contracts reduce invalid relay adoption compared with untyped artifact exchange.

**H6 — Interaction modeling:** Evaluating paired adaptations reveals consequential positive or negative interactions missed by component-only tests.

**H7 — Multi-perspective governance:** Constraint-aware portfolio selection reduces policy violations and severe regressions compared with unconstrained scalar optimization.

**H8 — Explicit Leftover:** Retaining failed and deferred change proposals improves later diagnosis and question generation compared with storing accepted changes alone.

**H9 — Repeated-execution relay:** Context-aware repeat records improve uncertainty calibration relative to treating repeated outputs as independent confirmations.

**H10 — Robustness:** Apparent benefits of coupled evolution persist on domain-held-out and temporally shifted evaluation sets; failure to persist falsifies broad generalization claims.

### 18.1 Falsification conditions

A claim of beneficial co-evolution is weakened or rejected if improvements vanish under independent evaluation, are explained by extra resource consumption, depend on leaked evaluation data, produce unacceptable governance violations, or cause persistent regressions in downstream systems.

## 19. Risks and Failure Modes

### 19.1 Ontology inflation

Too many UTN types may reduce alignment reliability and raise maintenance cost. Require evidence of utility and explicit deprecation/migration rules.

### 19.2 Feedback amplification

A biased scorer may promote questions that generate further confirming evidence, reinforcing its own error. Independent evaluators and counterevidence pathways are essential.

### 19.3 Goodhart effects

Optimizing accepted-change counts, influence scores, or question volume can displace genuine research value. Use multiple metrics and external validation.

### 19.4 Correlated evidence

Multiple agents may restate the same source. Provenance and evidence-dependency tracking are required before interpreting agreement as corroboration.

### 19.5 Semantic drift

A transfer may preserve names while changing meaning. Type contracts and target-domain tests must precede adoption.

### 19.6 Complexity without gain

Co-evolution may cost more than its benefits. A static or simpler architecture should remain a legitimate outcome of the experiments.

### 19.7 Authority escalation

Research proposals may recommend changing policy, but they do not grant the system authority to change its own strategic constraints, budgets, or deployment permissions.

### 19.8 Evaluation capture

If the same subsystem proposes changes and defines success, it can overfit or manipulate evaluation. Maintain independent, versioned evaluators and audit trails.

## 20. Governance and Human Research Sovereignty

AQSI may autonomously generate meta-questions and candidate structural adaptations. It must not treat this as permission to alter research priorities, execution boundaries, external systems, or evaluator definitions. The Strategic Question Control Plane should distinguish:

- **Discovery authority:** permission to propose questions.
- **Evaluation authority:** permission to run specified experiments.
- **Adoption authority:** permission to accept component changes.
- **Policy authority:** permission to revise goals, budgets, and constraints.

These authorities need not belong to the same actor. Sensitive changes should require explicit human approval and documented justification. Multi-agent consensus is not a substitute for authorization or scientific verification.

2. How should typed relationships evolve without fragmenting a shared vocabulary?
3. Which historical feedback is transferable when the underlying type system changes?
4. Can an influence model distinguish useful analogy from superficial structural similarity?
5. How should delayed benefits and negative externalities enter change acceptance decisions?
6. When is independent component tuning preferable to coupled adaptation?

8. How should research portfolios reserve resources for low-prior but potentially transformative questions?
9. Which relay artifacts are portable across institutions with different governance policies?
10. What evidence would justify claiming sustained co-evolution rather than a short sequence of local optimizations?
11. How can reviewers detect evaluator manipulation or hidden selection bias?
12. What should remain permanently under human strategic control as the system becomes more capable?

## 23. Research Roadmap

**Phase A — Contract inventory.** Define participant interfaces, UTN relation types, SCP fields, dependency edges, and evidence provenance. Establish frozen evaluation datasets.

**Phase B — Offline interaction study.** Reconstruct historical changes where available, test directional impact predictions, and quantify uncertainty and missingness.

**Phase C — Controlled co-adaptation.** Compare isolated and coupled adaptations on a small set of reversible changes; run factorial ablations and independent reviews.

**Phase D — Cross-domain relay.** Transfer validated change proposals and their evidence between two bounded domains; measure semantic compatibility and negative transfer.

**Phase E — Governed recursive research.** Permit repeated proposal–test–review cycles under explicit budgets, stopping criteria, and rollback policies. Report both successful and failed cycles.

Advance only when the prior phase meets predefined conformance and evidence thresholds. No phase implies an ASI trajectory.

---

## 24. Conclusion

Cross-Structural Co-Evolution proposes a way to study the reciprocal development of AQSI and its surrounding structural intelligence infrastructure. Questions can expose missing types, faulty scorers, incomplete relay contracts, and governance gaps; improved infrastructure can, in turn, make new questions representable and testable. The research opportunity lies in turning these interactions into **typed, evidence-bearing, reversible, and independently evaluated change cycles**.

The framework deliberately rejects three shortcuts: **more structural complexity is not automatically more intelligence; reciprocal feedback is not automatically improvement; and recursive modification is not automatically safe or open-ended progress.** Its value must be established through comparative experiments, transparent negative results, and sustained conformance to human-governed research boundaries.
